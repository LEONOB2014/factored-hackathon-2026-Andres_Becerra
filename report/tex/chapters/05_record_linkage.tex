\chapter{Record linkage and comparable rows}\label{ch:record-linkage}

\Cref{ch:inventory-keys,ch:time-and-clocks} established that most rows of the backup have no counterpart in the main
copy and that no re-dating aligns them. A minority of rows, however, can be paired, and on those pairs the remaining
hypothesis can be tested: that records present in both copies were silently changed (\ident{H5}). Pairing must be
earned row by row and validated against something the pairing did not use. This chapter builds the pairs in layers of
increasing ambition (dates of shared keys, people under different keys, products and agents by natural key,
transactions by content), then compares what the pairs say.

\section{Aligning the dates of shared keys}\label{sec:rl-dates}

For rows that share a key, \cref{ch:time-and-clocks} found offsets that drift smoothly by table. A drift model
predicts each pair's offset from the median offset of its month, estimated on the other months only (leave one month
out), so the evaluation is out of sample. The mean absolute error of the predicted date falls from
$\est{ch05.mae-raw-campaign-sends}{value}$ days with no correction to $\est{ch05.mae-drift-campaign-sends}{value}$ for
sends, against $\est{ch05.mae-const-campaign-sends}{value}$ for a single global constant, and from
$\est{ch05.mae-raw-transactions}{value}$ to $\est{ch05.mae-drift-transactions}{value}$ days for transactions, of which
$\est{ch05.within-day-transactions}{value}\,\%$ then fall within a day (\cref{tab:ch05-drift-alignment}). A residual
jitter of a day or more remains for events and sends: dates of shared keys can be aligned approximately, never exactly,
and day-level comparisons below use a tolerance window.

\section{People under different keys: probabilistic linkage}\label{sec:rl-fs}

\subsection{A shared identifier is a noisy label}

Of the $\est{ch05.shared-id}{value}$ customers whose key appears in both copies,
$\est{ch05.shared-id-all-four}{value}\,\%$ agree on document number, first name, last name and date of birth,
$\est{ch05.shared-id-none}{value}\,\%$ ($\est{ch05.shared-id-none-n}{value}$ customers) agree on none of them, and
$\est{ch05.shared-id-partial}{value}\,\%$ agree on some. A shared key does not mean a shared person: for
$\est{ch05.shared-id-none}{value}\,\%$ of the shared keys the key was given to someone else. Linkage therefore cannot be validated against keys.

\subsection{The Fellegi--Sunter model}

For a candidate pair $(a, b)$ of a main and a backup record, let $\gamma = (\gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_K)$ record whether
the pair agrees on each of $K$ comparison fields. The theory of \textcite{fellegi1969} compares the probability of the
agreement pattern among true matches $M$ and among non-matches $U$.

\begin{assumption}[Conditional independence of agreements]\label{ass:ci}
Given match status, agreements on different fields are independent:
$\Prob(\gamma \mid M) = \prod_k m_k^{\gamma_k}(1 - m_k)^{1 - \gamma_k}$ and
$\Prob(\gamma \mid U) = \prod_k u_k^{\gamma_k}(1 - u_k)^{1 - \gamma_k}$, with $m_k = \Prob(\gamma_k = 1 \mid M)$ and
$u_k = \Prob(\gamma_k = 1 \mid U)$.
\end{assumption}

Under \cref{ass:ci} the log-likelihood ratio, in bits, is a sum of field weights,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fs-weight}
  W(\gamma) = \sum_{k} \Bigl[\gamma_k \log_2 \frac{m_k}{u_k} + (1 - \gamma_k) \log_2 \frac{1 - m_k}{1 - u_k}\Bigr],
\end{equation}
and \textcite{fellegi1969} show that thresholding $W$ gives the linkage rule with the fewest undecided pairs for given
error rates. A field earns a large weight when matches agree on it and strangers rarely do.

\paragraph{Estimation.} Candidate pairs come from blocking: pairs that agree on the document number, on last name and
date of birth, on the mobile phone, or on e-mail and date of birth. There are $\est{ch05.candidates}{value}$ of them,
$\est{ch05.candidates-same-id}{value}$ sharing a key. The chance-agreement probabilities $u_k$ are estimated on random
pairs of main and backup customers, not on the candidates, because blocking forces agreement on the blocking fields.
The match probabilities $m_k$ and the match prevalence among candidates are then estimated by the EM algorithm
\autocite{dempster1977}, with $u$ held fixed, as in the census linkage of \textcite{jaro1989}.

The re-estimation differs from the exploratory notebook in one respect. The notebook drew its random pairs with an
unseeded sample, so its weights changed slightly from run to run; this report draws them deterministically (the
customers with the lowest seeded hash), so every rebuild reproduces the same weights (\cref{tab:ch05-fs-weights}). The
estimates agree with the notebook's: agreeing on the document number is worth $\est{ch05.w-document-number}{value}$ bits,
on the mobile phone $\est{ch05.w-mobile-phone}{value}$ and on the date of birth $\est{ch05.w-date-of-birth}{value}$,
while country ($\est{ch05.w-country}{value}$ bits) and gender ($\est{ch05.w-gender}{value}$) carry little, having few
categories.

Some estimates need a caveat. For the document number and the mobile phone no random pair agreed, and the estimate of
$u_k$ sits at the floor that keeps \cref{eq:fs-weight} finite. With no agreement among $N$ random pairs, an upper
confidence bound for $u_k$ is about $3/N$ at the conventional level \autocite{hanley1983}, far below the floor, so these
weights understate the true evidence and the linkage is, if anything, conservative. The EM prevalence among candidates
is $\est{ch05.em-prevalence}{value}$, the cap of the algorithm: candidates are enriched in matches by construction, so
absolute weights are approximate and the linkage is validated externally, below.

\subsection{What the scores show}

Because each field either agrees or not, the scores of \cref{eq:fs-weight} take values on a lattice of sums of field
weights (\cref{fig:ch05-scores}). Pairs that share a key form a high mode, the same person, and a low mode below the
threshold, the key given to someone else; most pairs under different keys cluster at a high score of their own,
agreeing on all identity fields but the contact details. Against the key as a label the score's AUC is only
$\est{ch05.auc-vs-id}{value}$, not because the score is weak but because the label is wrong for many of the shared
keys.

\subsection{Linkage and its blind validation}

At the exploratory notebook's threshold of $\est{ch05.cut}{value}$ bits, $\est{ch05.linked-people}{value}$ main-copy
customers are linked, $\est{ch05.linked-share}{value}\,\%$ of the population:
$\est{ch05.linked-same-id}{value}$ pairs under the same key and $\est{ch05.linked-other-id}{value}$ under a different
one. The result is stable around the threshold: it links $\est{ch05.linked-at-5}{value}$ customers at a much lower
threshold and $\est{ch05.linked-at-15}{value}$ at a higher one (\cref{tab:ch05-sensitivity}).

The linkage is validated on fields it never used (\cref{tab:ch05-validation}). Linked pairs agree on the address in

\section{Products, agents and transactions}\label{sec:rl-other}

Products carry a natural key, the product number, unique in each copy. Of the $\est{ch05.products-shared-id}{value}$
products that share a key, $\est{ch05.products-id-same-object}{value}\,\%$ also share their number. Pairing on the number
instead finds $\est{ch05.products-nk-pairs}{value}$ pairs, $\est{ch05.products-extra}{value}$ more than the shared keys,
so product linkage should use the number, not the key.

Transaction clones were identified in \cref{sec:time-filter} through shared keys. Searching without keys, for pairs
with an identical fingerprint whose date difference lies within a few days of the month's median offset, finds
$\est{ch05.window-pairs}{value}$ pairs and recovers $\est{ch05.window-recall}{value}\,\%$ of the clones found through
keys. It adds $\est{ch05.window-other-id}{value}$ pairs under different keys, but placebo windows displaced by several
months, where no true match can lie, return $\est{ch05.placebo-mean}{value}$ pairs on average: most of the additions are
chance, and the clone core is essentially the one the keys already reveal.

\section{What changed in the paired records}\label{sec:rl-changes}

\paragraph{Customers.} Customers that are the same person under the same key are identical in nearly every field;
the field that moves is the credit score. Among the $\est{ch05.scored}{value}$ of them with a score in both copies, $\est{ch05.rescored}{value}\,\%$
were re-scored, with a median absolute change of $\est{ch05.rescore-median-abs}{value}$ points and a mean change of
$\est{ch05.rescore-mean}{value}$: symmetric noise around the original. The change is not recorded. Re-scored
customers keep the same last-update timestamp in $\est{ch05.rescored-same-updated}{value}\,\%$ of cases, the same share
as customers whose score did not move ($\est{ch05.unchanged-same-updated}{value}\,\%$). In a bank, a value that changes
without its audit column is a change-control failure. Customers whose identity fields partly agree look like blends of
different records rather than updates: their city differs in $\est{ch05.edited-city-changed}{value}\,\%$ of cases.

\paragraph{Products.} Products paired by number keep their type, status and opening date, but
$\est{ch05.redenominated}{value}\,\%$ change currency, and the balance then changes by a fixed factor: a median of
$\est{ch05.rate-cop}{value}$ Colombian pesos and $\est{ch05.rate-ars}{value}$ Argentine pesos per dollar
(\cref{tab:ch05-redenomination}), flat rates that ignore the daily exchange-rate table. The re-denomination is exact in
the median, but the balances are not equal after it: the standard deviation of the decimal logarithm of the balance
ratio is between $\est{ch05.log-ratio-sd-min}{value}$ and $\est{ch05.log-ratio-sd-max}{value}$ for every currency
pair, including pairs that kept their currency. The backup holds a re-denominated and perturbed copy of the products'
economic state, attributed to different owners.

\paragraph{Transaction clones.} A clone is the same event, with the same amount, merchant, channel, status, fraud score
and time of day, posted to a different customer and product a few days later. It keeps the branch in
$\est{ch05.clone-same-branch}{value}\,\%$ of cases but the city in only $\est{ch05.clone-same-city}{value}\,\%$: the
city follows the new owner.

\paragraph{The control.} Complaints keep their keys in both copies and serve as the negative control. Of their
$\est{ch05.complaint-columns}{value}$ columns besides the key, $\est{ch05.complaint-columns-equal}{value}$ are equal in
every row; the others are the foreign keys to the customer, the product and the agent, and the customer agrees in
$\est{ch05.complaint-customer-equal}{value}\,\%$ of complaints. A method that flagged complaint content as changed would be
wrong; this one does not, and it shows that the backup re-keys foreign keys independently of attributes.

\section{Verdict and implications}\label{sec:rl-verdict}

\ident{H5}, silent mutation of shared records, is supported, but not as uniform noise. It has distinct components: an
untraced credit-score refresh, a flat-rate currency re-denomination with perturbed balances, and the re-attribution of
products and transactions to other customers. For the platform, keys are never trusted across sources without
validation: customers are linked probabilistically and validated on held-out fields, products by their natural key.
A source contract must require that every change of a value moves its audit timestamp, and that balances are
converted with the daily exchange-rate table. None of these changes affects a model gate, since every gate is computed
on the main copy alone; they decide what a backup can and cannot be used for, which is a restore of nothing but the
complaints.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:rl-limits}

\Cref{ass:ci} is false in detail: first name and gender, or city and country, are correlated, so the weights count
some evidence more than once. The blind validation is what guards against that. The candidate pairs come from blocking rules,
and a person whose record disagrees on every blocking field cannot be linked. The threshold is the exploratory
notebook's choice; the sensitivity table shows the conclusion does not depend on it.
