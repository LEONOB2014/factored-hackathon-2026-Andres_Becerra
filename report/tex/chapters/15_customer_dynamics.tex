\chapter{Customer dynamics}\label{ch:customer-dynamics}

A customer's relationship with the bank unfolds over months: active, quiet, active again, until it lapses. The
granularity series describes that movement with a Markov chain over monthly activity states, with survival analysis of the
time to the first long lapse, with the concentration of value across customers, and with cross-process early warnings.
This chapter examines the assumptions of each description and what each finds, with one question in front: how much of what
looks like customer behaviour is the exposure of \cref{sec:ag2-exposure}?

\section{Activity as a Markov chain}\label{sec:cd-markov}

Each customer-month is in one of the activity states: dormant (no transaction), light (at most $\est{ch15.light-max}{value}$) and
active (more). If the next state depends only on the current one, the sequence of states is a Markov chain with transition
matrix $P$, and its stationary distribution and sojourn times summarise it. The stationary distribution $\pi$, the solution of $\pi P = \pi$ with
$\sum_i \pi_i = 1$, is where the population settles if $P$ holds; the expected stay in state $i$ once entered is
$1 / (1 - P_{ii})$ months. Over $\est{ch15.transitions}{value}$ consecutive-month transitions (\cref{tab:ch15-markov}), the
stationary distribution puts $\est{ch15.pi-dormant}{value}$ of the population in the dormant state against
$\est{ch15.obs-dormant}{value}$ observed, $\est{ch15.pi-light}{value}$ in the light state against $\est{ch15.obs-light}{value}$
and $\est{ch15.pi-active}{value}$ in the active state against $\est{ch15.obs-active}{value}$: the bank is in equilibrium. A
dormant month is followed by another with probability $\est{ch15.p-dd}{value}$ and by activity with probability
$\est{ch15.p-da}{value}$, so a single empty month is weak evidence of a lapse; the dormant state lasts
$\est{ch15.sojourn-dormant}{value}$ months on average, the active state only $\est{ch15.sojourn-active}{value}$.

\paragraph{Is the chain Markov?} The exploratory notebook took the first-order property for granted. It can be tested by
asking whether the state of the month before last changes the next state once the current one is known \autocite{anderson1957}. With
$N_{ijk}$ the number of customer-month triples in states $i$, $j$, $k$, the likelihood-ratio statistic against a
second-order chain is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cd-g2}
  G^2 = 2 \sum_{i,j,k} N_{ijk} \log \frac{N_{ijk}\, N_{j\cdot}}{N_{ij\cdot}\, N_{jk}} ,
\end{equation}
with $N_{j\cdot}$, $N_{ij\cdot}$ and $N_{jk}$ the corresponding margins, asymptotically $\chi^2$ with
$\est{ch15.g2-df}{value}$ degrees of freedom for these states. Over $\est{ch15.triples}{value}$ triples it is
$\est{ch15.g2}{value}$ ($p = \est{ch15.g2-p}{value}$): the chain is not first-order. More informative than the test, which
rejects any departure at this sample size, is its size. Knowing the earlier state changes the probability of the next by
$\est{ch15.g2-effect}{value}$ on average (the largest change per current state, weighted by frequency). Within groups of
customers with a similar overall activity rate the same measure falls to a median of $\est{ch15.g2-effect-within-median}{value}$
and at most $\est{ch15.g2-effect-within-max}{value}$ across $\est{ch15.rate-groups}{value}$ groups.

The memory is therefore not in the customers; it is in the mixture. A population of customers with different rates, each
nearly memoryless, produces an aggregate sequence that looks as though it remembers: a customer seen dormant in consecutive months is more
likely to be one of the low-rate customers, and so to stay dormant. This is the mover--stayer structure of
\textcite{frydman1984}, and here its source is the exposure of \cref{sec:ag2-exposure}. The monthly chain is a sound KPI of
the population, its dormant-to-active and active-to-active rates monitored per market; it is not a model of an individual
unless it conditions on that individual's own rate.

\section{Dormancy and exposure}\label{sec:cd-dormancy}

Of customers active in a month, $\est{ch15.dormant-base}{value}$ make no transaction the next month. The series' model
predicts that with an AUC of $\est{ch15.model-auc}{value}$, its best single feature reaching $\est{ch15.model-best-single}{value}$.
The number of products a customer holds, alone and with no behaviour at all, reaches $\est{ch15.auc-held}{estimate}$
($\est{ch15.auc-held}{ci}$, DeLong); this month's number of transactions alone reaches only
$\est{ch15.auc-ntx}{estimate}$ ($\est{ch15.auc-ntx}{ci}$). Most of the learnable signal is exposure, as \cref{eq:ag2-exposure}
predicts, and the part that behaviour adds is small. A retention model on this data should be judged by its gain over the
products-held baseline, not over chance.

\section{Survival to the first lapse}\label{sec:cd-survival}

The series measures, from each customer's start, the time to the first run of $\est{ch15.lapse-months}{value}$ consecutive months without a transaction. Customers
still active at the end of the data are censored: their lapse has not been observed, and averaging only the lapsed customers'
times would understate the time to lapse. The Kaplan--Meier estimator handles censoring by multiplying conditional survival
over the observed lapse times,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cd-km}
  \hat S(t) = \prod_{t_i \le t} \Bigl(1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i}\Bigr),
\end{equation}
with $d_i$ lapses among the $n_i$ customers still at risk at time $t_i$ \autocite{kaplan1958}. Groups are compared by the
log-rank test \autocite{peto1972}, and summarised by the restricted mean survival time
$\mathrm{RMST}(\tau) = \int_0^\tau \hat S(t)\,dt$, the expected months engaged within a horizon $\tau$ \autocite{royston2013}.

On average $\est{ch15.lapsed-pct}{value}\,\%$ of customers lapse, with a median of $\est{ch15.median-lapse}{value}$ months and a
survival at a year between $\est{ch15.s12-min}{value}$ and $\est{ch15.s12-max}{value}$ in every group
(\cref{tab:ch15-survival}). The country difference is detectable (log-rank $p = \est{ch15.lr-country-p}{value}$) and
immaterial: the restricted mean survival times of the countries differ by $\est{ch15.rmst-gap}{value}$ of a month within $\est{ch15.rmst-horizon}{value}$ months. Segments do not differ ($p = \est{ch15.lr-segment-p}{value}$). The series' lapse-risk ranking, a proportional-hazards
model in the sense of \textcite{cox1972}, reaches a concordance of $\est{ch15.concordance}{value}$
($\est{ch15.concordance-lo}{value}$ to $\est{ch15.concordance-hi}{value}$) from activity in the first months, which is again a
measure of the customer's rate. Detectability and materiality part ways at this sample size; the restricted mean survival time,
in months a manager understands, is the measure to report.

\section{How concentrated is value?}\label{sec:cd-value}

The Lorenz curve plots the cumulative share of spend against the cumulative share of customers ordered by spend
\autocite{lorenz1905}, and the Gini coefficient is the area between it and the diagonal as a share of the area under the diagonal; for sorted spends
$x_{(1)} \le \dots \le x_{(n)}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cd-gini}
  G = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (2i - n - 1)\, x_{(i)}}{n \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{(i)}} .
\end{equation}
Lifetime spend has a Gini coefficient of $\est{ch15.gini}{value}$; the top tenth of customers account for
$\est{ch15.top-decile}{value}\,\%$ of it, and $\est{ch15.zero-spend}{value}\,\%$ of customers spent nothing. Value is moderately
concentrated: no small group carries the bank, and a service model tiered by value would have little to tier on.

\section{Early warnings across processes}\label{sec:cd-lifts}

The series tested whether an event in one process warns of another in the next month: a digital error of a contact, an
unresolved contact of a complaint, a complaint or a decline of dormancy, campaign pressure of attrition. For each pair it
compares the probability of the consequent with and without the antecedent (\cref{tab:ch15-lifts}). No relative risk differs
from one by more than $\est{ch15.lift-max-dev}{value}$, and the largest is the mechanical case of \cref{sec:ag2-ledger}: declined
customers are, by construction, active ones. The processes of this bank do not talk to each other at the customer-month
grain.

\section{Customers at the hour}\label{sec:cd-hours}

Does a customer have a preferred hour? For each customer with enough transactions, the series tested whether their hours
are uniform at a level of $\est{ch15.preferred-alpha}{value}\,\%$. If no customer had a preference, about that share would be
rejected by chance; the share rejected is $\est{ch15.preferred-hour}{pct}\,\%$ (Wilson interval
$\est{ch15.preferred-hour}{pct_ci}$), exactly the chance rate. The daypart profiles of customers are indistinguishable from
multinomial noise around a flat profile (Kolmogorov--Smirnov $p = \est{ch15.daypart-ks-p}{value}$). A personalised send time
has nothing to personalise on here.

\section{Verdict and implications}\label{sec:cd-verdict}

The customer dynamics of this bank are the dynamics of a mixture of customers with different rates. The population is in
equilibrium, a single quiet month is mostly noise, and the apparent memory of the monthly chain is the mixture's, not the
customers'. Dormancy and lapse are learnable mainly because customers differ in how many products they hold. Countries and
segments lapse alike in every way that matters, value is moderately spread, processes do not warn of one another, and nobody
prefers an hour. For the platform the useful deliverables are the population KPIs (the chain's rates per market, the
Kaplan--Meier curve and the restricted mean survival time) and a retention model judged against the exposure baseline.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:cd-limits}

The chain, the second-order test and the exposure baseline are recomputed here on processing months; the activity-rate groups
use each customer's rate over the whole window, a diagnostic of heterogeneity that a predictive model could not use. The
survival summary, the lapse model's concordance, the lifts and the hour tests are the series' stored results.
