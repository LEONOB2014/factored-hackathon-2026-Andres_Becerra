\chapter{Marts, rules and features without leakage}\label{ch:marts-and-features}

The gold layer serves consumers of different kinds. Marts answer operational questions with deterministic rules: is this
customer eligible for credit, how worried should collections be about this product, does this customer-month match a
money-laundering typology, which transaction is this complaint about? Feature tables feed learned models and must
contain, for every row, only what was knowable at the moment the row describes. This chapter examines both from the
built lakehouse: whether the features are free of leakage and contain anything to learn, and whether each rule behaves
as its design intends on this data.

\section{Point-in-time features}\label{sec:mf-pit}

A feature of an event at time $t$ for entity $e$ is point-in-time when it is a function of the entity's history strictly
before $t$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mf-pit}
  f_w(e, t) = A\bigl(\{\, x_{e,s} : t - w \le s < t \,\}\bigr),
\end{equation}
for a window $w$ and an aggregate $A$ (a count, a sum, a maximum, a time since the last event). The strict inequality
matters: including the current row lets a model read the event it is asked to predict. A feature that could not have been
computed at decision time is leakage, and leakage makes offline accuracy a promise production cannot keep
\autocite{kaufman2012}. The fraud features follow \cref{eq:mf-pit}: windows of an hour, a day and a week exclude the
current transaction, the last login is found by an as-of join on logins strictly before it, and the shipped fraud score,
which \cref{sec:ad-fraud} showed to be the label in disguise, is absent by design.

\paragraph{The out-of-time split.} Fraud models are trained, validated and tested on consecutive periods, never on a
random shuffle, because a shuffle lets a model learn the future of the very customers it is tested on. Between periods
an embargo removes the rows of a band of days before each boundary \autocite{lopezdeprado2018}: a rolling window that
straddles the boundary, or a label resolved after it, would otherwise carry information across. The split holds
$\est{ch11.rows-train}{value}$ training rows ($\est{ch11.fraud-train}{value}$ fraud), $\est{ch11.rows-valid}{value}$ validation
rows ($\est{ch11.fraud-valid}{value}$) and $\est{ch11.rows-test}{value}$ test rows ($\est{ch11.fraud-test}{value}$). The
measured gaps between the last training row and the first validation row, and between validation and test, are
$\est{ch11.gap-train-valid}{value}$ and $\est{ch11.gap-valid-test}{value}$ days, matching the configured embargo of
$\est{ch11.embargo-days}{value}$ days.

\section{Is there anything to learn?}\label{sec:mf-signal}

Before any model, the cheapest test of learnability is each feature alone. On the validation split, the ROC AUC of each of
the $\est{ch11.features}{value}$ numeric features against the fraud label, with DeLong intervals (\cref{eq:auc-delong}), is
at most $\est{ch11.auc-max-dev}{value}$ away from chance level (\cref{tab:ch11-single-auc}). Only
$\est{ch11.features-off-chance}{value}$ interval excludes chance, which is what chance alone produces at this number of
features, and that one is a feature almost constant across rows, the count of declines in the last day: its AUC differs
from chance level by $\est{ch11.auc-off-dev}{value}$, and its interval, $\est{ch11.auc-off-width}{value}$ wide, is narrow only
because the DeLong variance collapses when almost every score is tied. The features are well built and the label has no
learnable content, the same conclusion \cref{sec:ad-fraud} reached with a full model. No supervised fraud model should be
trained on this label.

\section{Credit eligibility}\label{sec:mf-credit}

Eligibility is a rule with reason codes: a customer is eligible unless a reason applies, and every refusal carries its
reasons, so that a copilot can explain a decision instead of reciting a score. Of $\est{ch11.elig-customers}{value}$
customers, $\est{ch11.elig-card}{value}$ are eligible for a credit card and $\est{ch11.elig-loan}{value}$ for a personal loan
(\cref{tab:ch11-reasons}). The dominant reason, insufficient income history, applies to
$\est{ch11.reason-income-share}{value}\,\%$ of customers. It follows from the direction assumption of the conformed fact
(\cref{ch:conformance}): only deposits count as inflows, so the income a rule can observe is thin. On real data with a
signed amount the same rule would see salaries; here it can only refuse, and it says why.

\section{The collections score}\label{sec:mf-collections}

The early-warning score adds points for warning signs: days past due, falling payments, declined payments for
insufficient funds, contacts, negative sentiment. An additive score ranks well only if its components separate the cases
that matter. By days-past-due bucket (\cref{tab:ch11-early-warning}), the mean score rises from
$\est{ch11.ew-current}{value}$ for current products to $\est{ch11.ew-dpd30}{value}$ for those up to a month late, and then
stops: $\est{ch11.ew-dpd60}{value}$ in the \ident{31-60} bucket and $\est{ch11.ew-dpd90}{value}$ in the \ident{90+} bucket. The
score cannot rank the delinquencies collections cares most about, because its other components are uninformative on this
data. And the $\est{ch11.ew-unknown-n}{value}$ products without a days-past-due value score like current ones
($\est{ch11.ew-unknown}{value}$): unknown is treated as on time. A score must separate unknown from good, by an explicit
state or by a separate flag, or it reports confidence it does not have.

\section{A peer rule that cannot fire}\label{sec:mf-aml}

The anti-money-laundering mart flags a customer-month whose inflow is extreme among its peers, the customers of the same
segment and country in the same month, by a robust $z$-score $(x - \tilde x) / (\kappa\,\mathrm{MAD})$ with the median and
the median absolute deviation of the peer group. In the built mart, the rule has fired $\est{ch11.aml-peer-hits}{value}$
times in $\est{ch11.aml-rows}{value}$ customer-months, and its score is defined for $\est{ch11.aml-z-defined}{value}$ of them.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:mf-mad}
If a strict majority of the values of a group equal $c$, the group's median is $c$ and its median absolute deviation is zero.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A strict majority of the values are at most $c$ and a strict majority are at least $c$, so every median equals $c$. The
absolute deviations from $c$ are zero for a strict majority of the values, so their median is zero.
\end{proof}

A customer-month without deposits has an inflow of zero, and $\est{ch11.aml-zero-share}{value}\,\%$ of customer-months have
none. Every one of the $\est{ch11.peer-groups}{value}$ peer groups has a majority of zeros (at least
$\est{ch11.peer-zero-min}{value}\,\%$), so by \cref{prop:mf-mad} every group has a MAD of zero, the division is guarded to
NULL, and the rule is dead. The breakdown is not a defect of the MAD: a high-breakdown estimator of scale must report zero
when a majority of the data coincide, and the more efficient alternatives of \textcite{rousseeuw1993} do the same. The fix
is to condition. Computed on the $\est{ch11.pos-rows}{value}$ months with inflow, the peer MAD is positive in every group and
the rule flags $\est{ch11.pos-flags}{value}$ customer-months. The lesson extends to every rule built on a robust scale: test
it on a planted positive before trusting its silence.

\section{Linking a complaint to a transaction}\label{sec:mf-disputes}

A dispute is about a transaction, but complaints carry no transaction key (\cref{ch:inventory-keys}). The disputes mart
links them by matching customer, amount and time. Of $\est{ch11.disputes}{value}$ complaints,
$\est{ch11.disputes-candidates}{value}$ have candidate transactions at all, $\est{ch11.disputes-unique}{value}$ have exactly
one, some have up to $\est{ch11.disputes-max}{value}$, and only $\est{ch11.disputes-linked}{value}$ reach a link with any
confidence. A heuristic cannot recover a key the source never recorded. The only real fix is to capture the disputed
transaction when the complaint is taken, which the copilot can do at intake.

\section{Campaigns: a causal question without a control}\label{sec:mf-campaigns}

The campaign mart asks whether a campaign caused conversions. In the potential-outcomes framework each send has an outcome
$Y(1)$ if the customer is treated and $Y(0)$ if not, and the uplift is $\tau = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0)]$
\autocite{rubin1974}. Only one outcome is ever observed for a unit, the fundamental problem of causal inference
\autocite{holland1986}, so $\tau$ is identified from observational data only if treated and untreated units are comparable
given observed covariates and every kind of unit has a positive chance of either. Here $\est{ch11.send-delivered}{value}\,\%$
of sends were delivered and the rest failed for reasons that may themselves relate to the customer; there is no untreated
group chosen at random, and conversions are rare ($\est{ch11.send-converted}{value}\,\%$). The uplift is not identifiable from
these data, and no model, however flexible, changes that; a randomised holdout must come before any uplift work.

The same mart answers compliance questions directly. $\est{ch11.send-no-consent}{value}\,\%$ of sends went to customers who do
not currently accept marketing, and $\est{ch11.send-already-held}{value}\,\%$ promoted a product the customer already held.
Gating on consent costs nothing measurable here, since conversions are rare either way, and is the first rule to enforce.

\section{Verdict and implications}\label{sec:mf-verdict}

The feature layer is built correctly: point-in-time windows, an embargoed out-of-time split, no leaked score. What it
feeds has nothing to learn from the fraud label. The marts are deterministic and explainable, which is their purpose, and
their behaviour on this data shows where each rule needs repair: eligibility refuses almost everyone for want of observed
income, the collections score saturates and mistakes unknown for current, the AML peer rule is dead by a theorem, dispute
linkage cannot replace a key, and campaign uplift needs an experiment the data never ran. Each finding is a change to a
rule or a requirement on the source, and none can be fixed by a better model.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:mf-limits}

The measurements read the lakehouse built by the last Airflow run; a rebuild after the fixes of earlier chapters would
change some counts. The complaint-in-ninety-days training table, which the notebooks propose as the first learned use case,
is a view onto files outside this workspace and could not be read. Single-feature AUCs bound what each feature carries
alone; interactions are bounded by the full model of \cref{sec:ad-fraud}.
