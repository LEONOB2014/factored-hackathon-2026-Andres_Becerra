\chapter{Are the main and backup copies the same process?}\label{ch:statistical-equivalence}

Rows of the copies cannot, in the main, be paired (\cref{ch:inventory-keys,ch:time-and-clocks,ch:record-linkage}). The
question left is whether they are draws from the same process: an independent replicate, the same generator with a
different random state (\ident{H3}). That is a question about distributions, and it needs inference. With as many rows as
these tables hold, every difference is statistically detectable, so the chapter calibrates every comparison against
the difference that the same process produces with itself, checks that the method detects what it should, and then
asks what the real differences are.

\section{Distances and their calibration}\label{sec:eq-distances}

For a numeric column with empirical distribution functions $F_m$ and $F_b$ in the copies, the exploratory notebook
used the Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance $D = \sup_x \lvert F_m(x) - F_b(x) \rvert$ \autocite{smirnov1948} and the
first Wasserstein distance $\int \lvert F_m(x) - F_b(x) \rvert\,dx$, divided by the main copy's standard deviation so
that it is scale-free \autocite{ramdas2017}. For a categorical column with category shares $p$ and $q$ it used
Cram\'er's $V$ of the copy-by-category table \autocite{cramer1946} and the Jensen--Shannon distance, the square root of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:js}
  \mathrm{JS}(p, q) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl(p \,\big\|\, \tfrac{p+q}{2}\bigr) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl(q \,\big\|\, \tfrac{p+q}{2}\bigr)
\end{equation}
in bits \autocite{lin1991}.

A distance alone says nothing about sameness: samples of the same process are never at distance zero. Each
distance is therefore divided by the distance between the parts of a random equal split of the main copy (leaving out rows that share a
key with the backup, which would make the copies look closer than they are). A ratio near one means the backup is as
close to the main copy as the main copy is to itself. Across the $\est{ch06.marginal-columns}{value}$ columns compared,
the core tables (customers, products, transactions) have median ratios between $\est{ch06.core-median-ratio-min}{value}$
and $\est{ch06.core-median-ratio-max}{value}$, and $\est{ch06.core-above-two}{value}$ of their columns exceeds the notebook's ratio threshold of
$\est{ch06.ratio-threshold}{value}$, the marketing-consent flag, whose distance is tiny in absolute terms (\cref{tab:ch06-marginals}). The other
tables have columns well above the baseline; \cref{sec:eq-differences} shows which.

\section{Joint structure: the classifier two-sample test}\label{sec:eq-c2st}

Columns that agree one at a time can still differ jointly, through their dependence. The classifier two-sample test
turns the question into prediction \autocite{lopezpaz2017}: label each row by its copy, train a classifier on all
non-key, non-date columns, and measure its cross-validated area under the ROC curve. If the copies come from the same
distribution, no classifier can beat chance, and the AUC stays at chance level up to sampling error; any joint difference a
flexible classifier can find raises it. The baseline is the same test between the parts of a random equal split of the main copy, whose AUCs lie
between $\est{ch06.auc-baseline-min}{value}$ and $\est{ch06.auc-baseline-max}{value}$ (\cref{fig:ch06-c2st,tab:ch06-c2st}).

Customers ($\est{ch06.auc-customers}{value}$), products ($\est{ch06.auc-products}{value}$) and transactions
($\est{ch06.auc-transactions}{value}$) are indistinguishable from the main copy even jointly. Digital events
($\est{ch06.auc-digital-events}{value}$) and campaign sends ($\est{ch06.auc-campaign-sends}{value}$) are slightly
separable, and contacts are almost perfectly separable ($\est{ch06.auc-call-center-interactions}{value}$). Complaints
fall below chance ($\est{ch06.auc-complaints}{value}$): their rows are identical in both copies, so every row of one
class has a twin in the other, and cross-validation then systematically scores the held-out twin against its own label.
An AUC below chance level here confirms identity; it is not evidence of a difference.

\section{Controls}\label{sec:eq-controls}

A comparison method is only credible if it detects a difference when there is one and stays silent when there is
none. The exploratory notebook ran both checks: complaints, identical in content, stay at the noise floor, and a small
mean shift injected into a random part of the main copy is detected by every distance. Both are positive and negative
controls on the method, not evidence about the bank (\cref{sec:em-ledger}).

\section{The paired main--backup estimator}\label{sec:eq-paired}

The notebook calibrated its comparisons on a single random equal split of the main copy's rows. Rows of one
customer are not independent (\cref{sec:em-clusters}), so this report adds an estimator that calibrates on the
independent unit.

\begin{definition}[Paired main--backup tolerance]\label{def:paired}
For a share $\theta$ computed in each copy, let $D = \hat\theta_b - \hat\theta_m$. Split the main copy's customers at
random into equal parts $A$ and $B$, $R$ times, and record $D^{0}_r = \hat\theta_{A_r} - \hat\theta_{B_r}$. With $n_m$ and $n_b$
the rows of the copies and $n_{A}$, $n_{B}$ those of the parts, the tolerance is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:paired-tol}
  \tau = Q_{1-\alpha}\bigl(\lvert D^{0} \rvert\bigr)\,
  \sqrt{\frac{n_m^{-1} + n_b^{-1}}{\overline{n_A^{-1} + n_B^{-1}}}},
\end{equation}
and the copies differ materially for $\theta$ when $\lvert D \rvert > \tau$.
\end{definition}

Splitting customers keeps every customer's rows together, so the noise in $D^{0}$ includes the dependence between
them; the square root rescales the spread between the parts to the sizes of the copies, since the variance of a
difference of shares is proportional to the sum of the reciprocal sizes. Under the hypothesis that the copies come
from the same process, $\lvert D \rvert$ exceeds $\tau$ with probability close to $\alpha$. The test suite checks this
on simulated clustered data: the false-alarm rate stays near $\alpha$, a real shift is detected, and the tolerance is
much wider than a row-level one when customers matter. The protocol freezes the same construction, applied to each
gate's metric, as the tolerance for comparing a gate's main and backup re-estimates (\cref{ch:readiness-gates}).

\paragraph{Applied to null rates.} The share of nulls in each column of the fact tables with a customer key gives
$\est{ch06.paired-columns}{value}$ comparisons. The paired tolerance flags $\est{ch06.paired-flags}{value}$ of them; a
row-level tolerance flags $\est{ch06.iid-flags}{value}$, and the notebook's row-level $z$-tests flagged
$\est{ch06.notebook-z-flags}{value}$. Here the customer correction changes little: the paired tolerance is, at the
median, $\est{ch06.widening-median}{value}$ times the row-level one, at most $\est{ch06.widening-max}{value}$ times.
Null rates in this dataset are set by column and channel, not by the customer, so rows of one customer are nearly
independent with respect to them, and the notebook's row-level test was adequate for this question. That is a finding,
not a disappointment: the estimator tells when the clustering correction matters and when it does not.

A flag measures detectability, not size. The flagged columns include differences of hundredths of a percentage point
alongside the large defects of \cref{sec:eq-differences} (\cref{tab:ch06-paired-flags}).

\section{The differences that are real}\label{sec:eq-differences}

Where the copies differ, they differ in a few columns, sharply. Contacts lose their call metrics in the backup: the
duration is null for $\est{ch06.null-main-duration}{value}\,\%$ of main rows and $\est{ch06.null-backup-duration}{value}\,\%$
of backup rows, against a tolerance of $\est{ch06.tol-duration}{value}$ percentage points, and the waiting time for
$\est{ch06.null-main-wait}{value}\,\%$ against $\est{ch06.null-backup-wait}{value}\,\%$. Campaign sends gain nulls in
the click flag, from $\est{ch06.null-main-clicked}{value}\,\%$ to $\est{ch06.null-backup-clicked}{value}\,\%$, and
digital events lose part of their marketing attribution. The vocabulary of the contact type changes: the backup labels
$\est{ch06.vocab-share}{value}\,\%$ of its contacts in Spanish where the main copy uses English, the only column of the
dataset whose vocabulary differs between the copies. An unmapped label would silently drop those contacts from every
rule that filters on the English value. These are targeted changes of a pipeline, not the diffuse differences of a
different population, and they are what the classifier of \cref{sec:eq-c2st} detects.

\section{Benford's law}\label{sec:eq-benford}

The leading digits of many naturally occurring amounts follow the logarithmic law
$\Prob(d) = \logten(1 + 1/d)$, $d \in \{1, \dots, 9\}$, first observed by \textcite{newcomb1881} and derived by
\textcite{hill1995} as the distribution that results from mixing many scales. Forensic accounting measures conformity by
the mean absolute deviation of the observed first-digit shares from the law \autocite{nigrini2012}. The transaction
amounts of the main copy have a deviation of $\est{ch06.benford-mad-main}{value}$ and those of the backup
$\est{ch06.benford-mad-backup}{value}$. Both sit at the bound above which Nigrini classes first digits as nonconforming
($\proto{benford.mad_first_digit_nonconformity}$), and above the bound for marginal acceptability
($\proto{benford.mad_first_digit_marginal}$). For the replicate hypothesis what matters is that the copies agree: they
deviate from the law in the same way and by almost the same amount, as samples of one generator would.

\section{Verdict and implications}\label{sec:eq-verdict}

\ident{H3} is supported for the core tables: customers, products and transactions are indistinguishable from the main
copy in every column and jointly, and their amounts share the same digit distribution. The backup is a re-draw of the
same generator, with targeted defects in contacts, sends and events. For the platform, the defects are exactly what a
data contract must catch: a vocabulary outside the declared values, a measure that turns null, a flag that loses its
values. \Cref{ch:schema-forensics} shows that the contract and its circuit breaker catch them. For the gates, the copies
can be compared only through distributions, never row by row, and the paired tolerance of \cref{def:paired} is the
yardstick with which \cref{ch:readiness-gates} judges whether a gate's backup re-estimate differs from its main one.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:eq-limits}

The distances, the classifier tests and the vocabulary and null scans are the exploratory notebook's, read from its
stored results; the paired estimator and the digit test are recomputed here. The classifier test detects differences
a gradient-boosting model can find with the columns given; a difference that only shows in a transformation the
classifier cannot learn would pass. The paired estimator splits by customer; for tables whose dependence runs through
another unit, such as the campaign or the day, it should split by that unit. Its tolerance uses a quantile estimated
from a finite number of splits, so ratios near one are uncertain.
