\chapter{Operations}\label{ch:operations}

Operations plan against forecasts and queues. How many transactions will a market handle next week, how much cash should a
branch load, how many agents does the contact centre need at this hour, which agents perform best, how long will a complaint
take? Each question has a classical method: rolling-origin forecasting, the newsvendor, Erlang C, reliability analysis,
survival with censoring. This chapter states each method's assumptions, checks them on the data, and reports what the
granularity series concluded with the uncertainty it left out.

\section{Forecasting the day}\label{sec:op-forecast}

\subsection{The backtest}

A forecast is judged by a rolling-origin backtest: at each origin, fit on the past only, forecast the next $h$ days and score
the errors \autocite{hyndman2006}. The series uses $h = \est{ch16.fc-h}{value}$, an origin every $\est{ch16.fc-step}{value}$ days
over the last $\est{ch16.fc-test}{value}$ days, and the mean absolute scaled error,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op-mase}
  \mathrm{MASE} = \frac{\frac{1}{m} \sum_{t} \lvert y_t - \hat y_t \rvert}
                       {\frac{1}{n - 7} \sum_{s=8}^{n} \lvert y_s - y_{s-7} \rvert},
\end{equation}
which divides the forecast's error by the in-sample error of the forecast "same weekday last week": below one, the model beats
that seasonal-naive rule. Recomputed here on each market's daily transactions and contacts, the calendar regression of
\cref{eq:cp-calendar} has a MASE between $\est{ch16.fc-mase-min}{value}$ and $\est{ch16.fc-mase-max}{value}$ and beats the naive
forecast in $\est{ch16.fc-beats-naive}{value}$ of $\est{ch16.fc-series}{value}$ series (\cref{tab:ch16-forecasts}). Its
prediction intervals are less reliable than its point forecasts: the series' nominal $\est{ch16.cov-nominal}{value}\,\%$ intervals covered between
$\est{ch16.cov80-min}{value}\,\%$ and $\est{ch16.cov80-max}{value}\,\%$ of outcomes.

\subsection{Is the gain real? The test and its variance}

Whether a lower MASE is more than luck is judged by the Diebold--Mariano test \autocite{diebold1995}: with loss differential
$d_t = \lvert e^{\text{model}}_t \rvert - \lvert e^{\text{naive}}_t \rvert$, the statistic $\bar d / \sqrt{\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\bar d)}$
is compared with a $t$ distribution after the small-sample correction of \textcite{harvey1997}. The variance is where the
care lies.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:op-dm}
Errors of forecasts made from the same origin at horizons $1, \dots, h$ share that origin's information, so the loss
differentials form, at best, a moving average of order $h - 1$, and
$\operatorname{Var}(\bar d) = \frac{1}{m}\bigl(\gamma_0 + 2 \sum_{k=1}^{h-1} \gamma_k\bigr)$ with $\gamma_k$ the
autocovariances of $d_t$.
\end{proposition}

The notebooks compute the test with $\gamma_0$ alone, which is correct only for one-step forecasts. With
$\est{ch16.fc-h}{value}$-day horizons the autocovariances belong in the variance; a Newey--West estimator with declining
weights keeps it positive \autocite{newey1987}. And because only $\est{ch16.fc-origins}{value}$ origins supply the
$\est{ch16.fc-errors}{value}$ errors of each series, a test on the mean differential of each origin block, with that many
degrees of freedom, is the most honest of all. The versions find $\est{ch16.fc-sig-naive}{value}$,
$\est{ch16.fc-sig-nw}{value}$ and $\est{ch16.fc-sig-block}{value}$ series better than naive at the conventional level, but not
the same ones, and the $p$-values of the Newey--West version range from $\est{ch16.fc-p-ratio-min}{value}$ to
$\est{ch16.fc-p-ratio-max}{value}$ times those of the notebooks' version. The calendar model is better on average in every
series; the evidence for any single series is weak and depends on a choice the notebooks did not make explicitly. A longer
backtest, with more origins, is the remedy.

\paragraph{Does one process lead another?} A series $x$ Granger-causes $y$ when past values of $x$ improve the forecast of $y$
beyond $y$'s own past \autocite{granger1969}. The series tested $\est{ch16.granger-tests}{value}$ pairs of processes (errors
before contacts, contacts before complaints, declines before contacts, sends before transactions). The smallest $p$-value is
$\est{ch16.granger-p-min}{value}$ and the smallest Benjamini--Hochberg $q$-value $\est{ch16.granger-q-min}{value}$; no pair is
a discovery. The stored adjusted values are all one ($\est{ch16.granger-stored-one}{value}$ of them), which is not the
Benjamini--Hochberg adjustment, but the conclusion does not depend on it: no process of this bank leads another by days.

\section{Branch cash}\label{sec:op-cash}

A branch must load cash before it knows the day's withdrawals. Forecasting each branch's daily cash $\est{ch16.fc-h}{value}$ days ahead,
gradient boosting reaches a median MASE of $\est{ch16.cash-mase-boost}{value}$ against $\est{ch16.cash-mase-naive}{value}$ for
the naive forecast. Forecasts at different levels of a hierarchy need not agree: on average over the markets, summing branch forecasts gives a market forecast with a mean absolute error of $\est{ch16.hier-market-bu}{value}$ dollars, against $\est{ch16.hier-market-td}{value}$ for
forecasting the market directly, while at the branch level the direct forecasts ($\est{ch16.hier-branch-bu}{value}$) beat the
market split down ($\est{ch16.hier-branch-td}{value}$). Each level is forecast best at its own level, and reconciliation methods
combine them into coherent forecasts that use the information of both \autocite{hyndman2011,wickramasuriya2019}.

How much to load is a newsvendor problem \autocite{arrow1951}. If a dollar short costs $c_u$ and a dollar idle costs $c_o$, the
expected cost is minimised by loading the quantile
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op-newsvendor}
  q^{\star} = F^{-1}\Bigl(\frac{c_u}{c_u + c_o}\Bigr)
\end{equation}
of the demand distribution $F$, here the forecast plus a buffer from the quantiles of past errors. Over the backtest
(\cref{tab:ch16-cash}), the cheapest of the $\est{ch16.cash-policies}{value}$ policies covers demand on
$\est{ch16.cash-best-sl}{value}\,\%$ of branch-days; cutting the buffer saves idle cash and loses more in shortfall. The costs
themselves are assumptions of the notebook, and the service level that follows from them should be set with the business.

\section{Staffing the contact centre}\label{sec:op-erlang}

Erlang C models the contact centre as an $M/M/c$ queue: Poisson arrivals at rate $\lambda$, exponential handle times with mean
$\mathrm{AHT}$, and $c$ agents \autocite{gross2008}. With offered load $A = \lambda \cdot \mathrm{AHT}$ in Erlangs, the probability
that a contact waits is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op-erlangc}
  P_W = \frac{\dfrac{A^c}{c!} \dfrac{c}{c - A}}{\displaystyle \sum_{k=0}^{c-1} \frac{A^k}{k!} + \frac{A^c}{c!} \frac{c}{c - A}},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{SL}(t) = 1 - P_W\, e^{-(c - A)\, t / \mathrm{AHT}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{SL}(t)$ is the share answered within $t$ seconds. For the bank's average hour of inbound calls, with
$\lambda = \est{ch16.lambda-hour}{value}$ calls an hour and a handle time of $\est{ch16.aht}{value}$ seconds, the load is
$\est{ch16.load}{value}$ Erlangs. The standard target of $\est{ch16.target-share}{value}\,\%$ answered within
$\est{ch16.target-s}{value}$ seconds needs $\est{ch16.agents-80-20}{value}$ agents, who answer $\est{ch16.sl-4}{value}\,\%$ in time,
while one fewer answers only $\est{ch16.sl-3}{value}\,\%$.

The first assumption holds here: hourly arrivals are Poisson (\cref{sec:cp-hours}). The second does not. Handle times range
from $\est{ch16.aht-min}{value}$ to $\est{ch16.aht-max}{value}$ seconds with a coefficient of variation of
$\est{ch16.aht-cv}{value}$, where the exponential's is one. Less variable service means shorter queues; the Allen--Cunneen
approximation scales the $M/M/c$ mean wait by $(1 + C_s^2)/2 = \est{ch16.ac-factor}{value}$ for service coefficient of
variation $C_s$ \autocite{allen1990}. With $\est{ch16.agents-fewer}{value}$ agents the mean wait falls from
$\est{ch16.wq-mmc-3}{value}$ to about $\est{ch16.wq-mgc-3}{value}$ seconds, with $\est{ch16.agents-80-20}{value}$ from
$\est{ch16.wq-mmc-4}{value}$ to about $\est{ch16.wq-mgc-4}{value}$. Erlang C is therefore conservative on this data: a roster
planned with it errs towards too many agents, a known and usually acceptable bias, but one worth knowing when a target is
tight. The method transfers: forecast arrivals per hour, measure the handle-time distribution, and size with the model that
fits it.

\section{Agents and shifts}\label{sec:op-agents}

A league table of agents ranks them by resolution rate, escalations, handle time, sentiment and survey score. It is meaningful
only if the KPI measures something stable about the agent. Split each agent's contacts at random into equal parts; if the KPI is a property of the agent, its values on the parts correlate, with correlation $r$, and the reliability of the KPI on all contacts
is the Spearman--Brown projection $2r / (1 + r)$ \autocite{spearman1910,brown1910}. A random-intercept model gives the same
quantity as the share of variance between agents, the intraclass correlation \autocite{shrout1979}. Across
$\est{ch16.agents}{value}$ agents, no KPI has a reliability above $\est{ch16.sb-max}{value}$ in absolute value, and the intraclass
correlations are at most $\est{ch16.icc-max}{value}$ (\cref{tab:ch16-agents}). The league table is noise: ranking agents by it
would reward and punish luck. Shift labels are equally empty. Agents labelled for a morning, afternoon or night shift handle
between $\est{ch16.shift-inside-min}{value}\,\%$ and $\est{ch16.shift-inside-max}{value}\,\%$ of their contacts inside it, the
share of the day a shift covers: the label is unrelated to when they work.

\section{Complaint cases and the clock}\label{sec:op-cases}

Only $\est{ch16.case-resolved}{value}\,\%$ of the $\est{ch16.case-n}{value}$ complaint cases are resolved by the end of the data.
The mean time to resolution of the resolved cases is $\est{ch16.case-naive-mean}{value}$ days, a figure that describes the
minority and invites the reading that cases close in about a fortnight. The Kaplan--Meier estimate (\cref{eq:cd-km}), which
counts the open cases as censored, says instead that $\est{ch16.case-p30}{value}\,\%$ of cases are resolved within a month, and its median is undefined because a majority of cases is never resolved. Ignoring censoring reverses the story.

The breach of the service level cannot be foreseen at assignment: a classifier reaches an AUC of $\est{ch16.sla-auc}{value}$
($\est{ch16.sla-auc-lo}{value}$ to $\est{ch16.sla-auc-hi}{value}$). The flag itself does not follow the regulatory clock it is
meant to record: its agreement with breaches recomputed from the timestamps and each country's business hours, measured by
Cohen's $\kappa$, the agreement beyond chance \autocite{cohen1960}, is at most $\est{ch16.kappa-max}{value}$ in absolute value.
Neither the hour a case arrives (Kruskal--Wallis $p = \est{ch16.kw-hour-p}{value}$) nor its priority (log-rank
$p = \est{ch16.lr-priority-p}{value}$) changes how fast it is answered. In this data the service-level flag is a random label and
the priority field changes nothing; on real data both are what a regulator would audit first.

\section{The hour of operations}\label{sec:op-hours}

The intraday profile of every channel in every market is flat: all $\est{ch16.channel-flat}{value}$ of
$\est{ch16.channel-tests}{value}$ channel-market profiles are consistent with uniform hours, with effect sizes $w$ of at most
$\est{ch16.channel-w-max}{value}$, below what \textcite{cohen1988} calls small. Branch transactions ignore opening hours: on
weekdays $\est{ch16.teller-out-obs}{value}\,\%$ of teller transactions fall outside the schedule against
$\est{ch16.teller-out-blind}{value}\,\%$ if the schedule were ignored, and over all days $\est{ch16.teller-every-obs}{value}\,\%$
against $\est{ch16.teller-every-blind}{value}\,\%$. Staffing by hour, opening hours and peak management cannot be calibrated on
this data.

\section{Verdict and implications}\label{sec:op-verdict}

The operational methods are the right ones and they are implemented correctly; the data give most of them little to work on.
Daily volumes are forecastable to a modest degree, with evidence per series that is weaker than the notebooks report once the
test's variance is right; no process leads another; cash forecasts beat naive and the newsvendor sets the buffer; Erlang C is
conservative because handle times vary less than it assumes; agent KPIs, shift labels, priorities and the service-level flag
carry no information; hours are flat and opening hours are ignored. What transfers to a real bank is the method with its
checks: forecast with a backtest whose test uses the horizon's variance, size queues with the handle-time distribution that is
measured, and test a KPI's reliability before publishing a league table.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:op-limits}

The forecast backtest is recomputed here with the smaller calendar model of \cref{ch:change-points} on processing days; the
notebooks' fuller calendar and their other models are not refitted. The staffing example uses the bank's average hour, not a
per-market roster. The cash, agent, case, clock and hour results are the series' stored results.
